A single glove, dropped on a footpath, usually ends up somewhere odd: on a
fence post, balanced on a bollard, propped in the fork of a low branch.
Nobody who does this owns the glove, and nobody who does it will ever meet
its owner. This week's question: why does a stranger bother? Last week the
Office opened on your own deposit. This week the object belongs to someone
else, and the question turns from losing to handing in.

## The fence-post economy

Putting a glove somewhere visible costs the finder almost nothing — a few
seconds, no form, no queue — and it raises the odds the owner walks back
along the same path and spots it. That is the whole transaction: minimum
effort, offered on the chance it matters to someone. An informal
lost-and-found built entirely out of small, unpaid gestures like this
covers far more lost objects than any staffed counter ever will, simply
because it needs no counter to run.

## What people do when nobody is watching

Cohn, Maréchal, Tannenbaum and Zünd tested this directly at a much larger
scale: researchers "lost" thousands of wallets, with varying amounts of
cash inside, at institutions across forty countries, and tracked how many
were reported back. Return rates were consistently high, and rose rather
than fell as the amount of money in the wallet went up. People were not
handing wallets in because it cost them nothing; the wallets with money in
them were harder to ignore, not easier to keep. Handing in seems to be
driven less by what a stranger can gain than by what they cannot avoid
noticing once they have picked the object up.

The Office cannot build a fence post into its hand-in point, but it can pay
attention to what the fence post gets right: a place to leave something
that takes no more effort than the fence post did, sited where people
already pass.

## Reading

Cohn, Maréchal, Tannenbaum & Zünd (2019), "Civic honesty around the globe",
*Science*. Read it for the actual numbers behind "people mostly hand things
in", measured rather than assumed.

## What the Office gains

A hand-in point, designed and sited by the class this week, together with
the rules for what a stranger can leave there and how.

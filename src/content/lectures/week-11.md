Every object this course has handled so far has waited to be found. A
dog does not wait; it can smell its own way home, approach a stranger, or
turn up three suburbs away at another shelter entirely. Week 10 closed
the pipeline on objects nobody comes back for; this week asks what
changes when the lost thing is looking for you too: what if it is
searching as hard as you are?

## What actually finds a lost dog

Lord, Wittum, Ferketich, Funk and Rajala-Schultz surveyed how owners
actually recovered lost dogs and which methods worked. Posters, calling
shelters and walking the neighbourhood all had a role, but a dog wearing
identification and a microchip on record was recovered far more reliably
than one relying on an owner's search effort alone. The finding cuts
against the intuition that searching harder is what brings a dog home —
the identification fixed to the animal before it went missing did most
of the work.

## Registries that cannot see each other

A microchip is only useful if whoever finds the dog can reach the
registry it is recorded on, and there is more than one registry, run by
different organisations that do not share records. A dog scanned at one
shelter can be invisible to the registry an owner actually reported it
to. This is not a hardware failure; the chip worked exactly as designed.
It is a matching failure between two systems that each hold half the
answer and never compare notes.

## The form problem, at the scale of two Offices

Week 3 asked whether a description can be written down so a stranger
understands it; matching a found dog to a lost-dog report is the same
problem between two forms that were never designed to agree with each
other. The same gap opens whenever two tutorials' Offices try to match a
depositor's intake form against another Office's list of unclaimed
objects — an inconsistent field, a missing category, a different unit,
and a genuine match fails to surface even though both records describe
the same thing.

## Reading

Lord, Wittum, Ferketich, Funk & Rajala-Schultz (2007), "Search and
identification methods that owners use to find a lost dog", *JAVMA*.
Read it for what worked before assuming a wider search is always the
answer.

## What the Office gains

A matching exercise with another tutorial's Office: intake forms
swapped, and a count of how many genuine matches the two forms' own
disagreements caused to be missed.

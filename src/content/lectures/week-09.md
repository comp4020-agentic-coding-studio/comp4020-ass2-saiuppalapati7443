Dentures turn up in lost property more often than most people expect,
and are reclaimed less often than almost anything else on the shelf.
Week 8 asked who owns a thing nobody claims; this week asks the question
that comes before ownership even becomes relevant: why do people not
come back for their things?

## Embarrassment keeps a claim from being made

Some things are inconvenient to describe out loud to a stranger at a
counter. Dentures are an easy example, but the same holds for anything a
person would rather quietly replace than admit, in detail, to losing.
The Office cannot fix embarrassment with a better form; it can only
notice that its intake and claim procedures were built assuming
claimants find describing their own losses easy, and some do not.

## The cost of claiming can outweigh the thing

Retrieving a claim can cost more than the object is worth: a trip across
campus, a wait at the counter, a form to fill in, all spent on
recovering something replaceable for a few dollars. Week 2 showed that
the cost of handing something in decides what a finder bothers to bring
to the Office at all. This week is that argument's mirror: the cost of
claiming decides what a rightful owner bothers to collect, and the two
costs are paid by different people at opposite ends of the same
transaction.

## Not knowing where to look is its own failure

A claimant who does not know the Office exists, or does not know their
object went there rather than somewhere else, cannot be deterred by cost
or embarrassment — they never reach the decision at all. An audit of
what is sitting unclaimed is, among other things, a record of every
place the Office's own visibility failed before the cost of claiming
ever became the issue.

## Reading

No new source this week. Return to Cohn, Maréchal, Tannenbaum & Zünd
(2019), the civic-honesty study behind week 2, and read it again for
what it implies about the claimant's side of the transaction rather than
the finder's — the same study describes both halves of the same office.

## What the Office gains

An audit of what nobody has claimed: every deposit still on the shelf,
how long it has sat there, and a first guess at which of the three
failures — embarrassment, cost, or not knowing where to look — explains
it.

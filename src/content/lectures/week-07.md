A teddy bear is worth nothing at resale and, more reliably than a suitcase
or a wallet, the exact thing a claimant will not stop calling about. Week
6 asked how the Office proves a claimant owns something; this week's
question comes before that one: who decides what is worth keeping at all?

## Market value and worth are not the same number

A secondhand bear, torn ear and all, sells for less than the bag it might
be carried home in. That number describes what a stranger would pay for
it, not what it is worth to the person who lost it. Winnicott's work on
transitional objects gives the Office a reason for the gap rather than
just a shrug at it: a soft toy a child has slept with stands in for a
security no price tag captures, and an adult claimant chasing down a worn
bear is not being irrational about money — they were never pricing it in
the first place.

## A thing's value changes as it moves

Kopytoff's argument is that an object does not carry one fixed worth
through its life; it acquires a different value at each stage of its
biography. The same bear is a child's toy, then a dropped object, then an
entry on a shelf waiting to be triaged, then — if the Office does its job
— a reunited possession again. Valuing it correctly means recognising
which stage it is in, not settling on a single number and defending it.

## The shelf from week 5 cannot hold everything

Week 5 built shelving on the assumption that everything deposited would
fit somewhere. It will not always. When the shelf is full, keeping one
low-value, high-worth object over another means something left, sold or
thrown away — so valuing, for the Office, is not an estimate but a
choice, made under a constraint the class can see: there is only so much
shelf.

## Reading

Winnicott (1953), "Transitional Objects and Transitional Phenomena"; and
Kopytoff (1986), "The cultural biography of things", in Appadurai (ed.),
*The Social Life of Things*. Read either for a reason a bear can outrank
a suitcase in the queue for shelf space, price notwithstanding.

## What the Office gains

Triage rules: the criteria the Office uses to decide, once the shelf runs
out of room, what stays and what does not — weighing sentimental worth
against market value on purpose, instead of by accident.

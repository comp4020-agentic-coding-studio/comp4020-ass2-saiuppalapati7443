Week 7 asked what a thing is worth keeping; a wedding ring, engraved and
worth keeping by anyone's measure, raises a harder question once no
owner turns up to claim it: who owns a thing whose true owner cannot be
found?

## The finder beats everyone except the true owner

*Armory v Delamirie* (1722) settled the baseline centuries before this
course existed: a chimney sweep's boy who found a jewel had a claim good
against everyone in the world except its true owner, including the
jeweller who tried to keep it. Finding something does not make you its
owner, but it puts you ahead of every other claimant who did not find it
— including, crucially, the Office, which is only ever holding the ring
on somebody's behalf.

## Where it was found can beat who found it

*Bridges v Hawkesworth* (1851) gave a customer who found banknotes on a
shop's public floor the right to keep them over the shopkeeper's
objection — the floor was open to anyone, so the shop had no special
claim to what turned up on it. *Parker v British Airways Board* (1982)
drew the line the other way: an occupier only outranks a finder if they
can show a manifest intention to control the place and what turns up in
it, not just ownership of the building. *Hannah v Peel* [1945] sits
alongside both — a requisitioned house whose owner had never actually
occupied it, so the finder's claim stood. None of the three turns on how
valuable the find was; all three turn on who controlled the place it was
found.

## The Office is not a court

These four cases are English common law, not this course's jurisdiction
by default. Look up what your own jurisdiction actually says about
finders, occupiers and unclaimed property before assuming any of it
applies here — the Office's own terms and conditions have to state a
position regardless of what a court would eventually decide, because a
ring cannot sit on the shelf forever while that question gets resolved.

## Reading

*Armory v Delamirie* (1722); *Bridges v Hawkesworth* (1851); *Hannah v
Peel* [1945]; *Parker v British Airways Board* [1982]. Read for the
occupier/finder line, not for an answer that transfers to your own
jurisdiction unread.
